What it is

A physical therapy technique.

Dry needling uses a thin, sterile, single-use needle that is inserted through the skin into selected muscle or connective tissue. No medication or fluid is injected; the word “dry” refers to the absence of an injection.

A physical therapist selects treatment areas after examining symptoms, movement, strength, and tissue sensitivity. The technique may be used to address pain, muscle guarding, or limited movement, but individual responses vary.

Dry needling is generally one component of care rather than a complete treatment by itself. It may be combined with mobility work, strengthening, movement retraining, and education.

Treatment is optional. The therapist should explain the proposed technique, alternatives, potential benefits, and risks before asking for consent.

Possible applications

Why it may be
considered.

A clinician may consider dry needling when muscle sensitivity, guarding, or restricted movement appears relevant to a patient’s symptoms. It is not appropriate for every person or every condition.

01Neck & shoulder tension

Persistent tightness, tender trigger points, or motion that feels restricted.

02Low back & hip pain

Muscle guarding that makes bending, lifting, sitting, or walking harder.

03Sports overload

Calf, hamstring, quad, or shoulder tightness that limits training and recovery.

04Headache-related tension

Sensitive muscles around the neck and shoulders that may contribute to symptoms.

05Post-surgical stiffness

When cleared and appropriate, as one part of restoring comfortable movement.

06Movement limitations

Areas that stay guarded despite stretching, exercise, or hands-on treatment.

What to expect

From assessment to follow-up.

Assessment and screening

The therapist reviews symptoms, goals, movement, health history, medications, precautions, and whether dry needling is appropriate.

Explanation and consent

The therapist explains the proposed treatment, alternatives, possible sensations, and risks. A patient may pause or decline treatment at any time.

Needle placement

Treatment is usually brief. Sensations may include a pinch, pressure, ache, muscle twitch, or cramp-like feeling.

Reassessment

The therapist checks the response and may follow with movement, mobility, or strengthening based on the treatment plan.

Electrical stimulation

When needles are paired with current.

Electrical stimulation with dry needling—sometimes called intramuscular electrical stimulation—uses lead wires to connect placed needles to a stimulation device. The device sends a controlled, low-level electrical current between needles.

Depending on the settings, a patient may feel light tapping, pulsing, or repeated muscle contractions. The intensity is adjusted gradually and should remain within the patient’s tolerance.

The therapist may consider this approach when a longer or more consistent stimulus is useful for the treatment goal. It is not automatically added to every dry needling session. Health history, implanted electrical devices, sensation, treatment location, patient preference, and other precautions must be reviewed first.

Electrical stimulation is discussed separately and used only with the patient’s consent.

Safety first

Clear. Clean. Optional.

Dry needling is performed by a trained physical therapist using sterile, single-use needles and clean technique. Before treatment, tell us about your medical history and anything that may affect safety.

Blood-thinning medication or bleeding disorders
Pregnancy or possible pregnancy
Needle anxiety or a history of fainting
Active infection, skin changes, or recent surgery
Implanted devices or significant medical conditions
Temporary soreness, bruising, fatigue, or a brief symptom flare can occur. Your therapist will review expected responses and when to seek care.

Questions, answered

Before your first visit.

Is dry needling the same as acupuncture?

No. Both use thin needles, but dry needling is based on a physical therapy examination and targets muscles and movement impairments. Acupuncture uses a different assessment and treatment system.

Does dry needling hurt?

Most people feel a brief pinch, pressure, ache, or muscle twitch. Some post-treatment soreness is possible and often feels similar to exercise soreness.

What is electrical stimulation with dry needling?

After needles are placed, lead wires may be connected to a device that delivers a controlled, low-level current. This may feel like tapping, pulsing, or muscle contractions. It is used only when appropriate after screening and consent.

How many sessions will I need?

There is no standard number for everyone. The therapist reassesses symptoms and function and continues the technique only when it contributes to the treatment plan.

Can I exercise afterward?

Often, yes. Gentle movement and hydration are commonly encouraged. Your therapist will give guidance based on the area treated and your individual response.

Can I receive dry needling without other physical therapy?

Dry needling works best as part of a plan. We pair it with the education and exercise needed to address the movement problem—not only the sore spot.
